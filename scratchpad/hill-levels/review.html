Bridges in the kit's stone and timber

Every image is the game's own renderer at zoom 4 (stacking at zoom 3). Click an image for full size. Nothing here is the default yet: the game still draws the kit until you pick.

  • Textured: the procedural shapes you liked, built face by face. The surfaces are taken from your kit images: each painted face is flattened back to a rectangle and repeated at a fixed scale. So a pier keeps its stone courses at any height instead of stretching, and planks stay plank-sized.
  • Stone uses the kit's coursed pier blocks for walls and its flagstones for tops. Timber uses the kit's planks for decks and the post's grain for posts, braces and rails.
  • Over water, stone becomes a viaduct with one arch per tile, and timber a braced trestle standing in the water. Over land, both stand on piers cut to the ground.

Stacking: building a platform onto a platform raises it

A river cuts through a ridge at level 2. Ground next to water is never more than one level up, so the banks drop to level 1 at the water's edge. The three cases below are the same ground, bridged three ways. In the profiles, the bars show the ground (blue is water), the line shows the rail and the hatching shows bridge platforms. Each platform built onto an existing one raises that tile's deck a level, here to 3. The rails climb onto it and back down like any incline.

Two ways to count the stack

1 · Stack adds to the bank rule (recommended). One platform behaves exactly as today; every extra platform on the same tile adds a level, up to level 4. Existing saves and bridges do not change.

2 · Stack counts up from the ground. The deck stands at the ground under it plus one level per extra platform, and the bank rule goes away. In case B you would need three platforms on each water tile to reach level 2. That is more explicit but costlier, and it changes existing bridges.

Either way, the rails on and off a stacked bridge follow the incline rules: a straight line climbs at most one level per tile. Curves and switches on a stacked pad sit at its deck. Bridges today have an upgrade level that raises their weight capacity. Stacking would sit beside it, not replace it.

Found while rendering

Raised decks and things standing behind them

The deck and supports draw in a layer beneath trees, reeds and buildings. On a raised deck, a reed growing on the tile in front correctly overlaps the deck foot. But a tree standing behind a high deck draws over it too. The full implementation sorts bridge supports with the objects around them, keeping the rails above the deck.
